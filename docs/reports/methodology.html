Methodology

Research Design, Sample Construction, and Analytical Decisions

This page documents how Island Time, Interrupted was designed and conducted: how the candidate universe and analytical samples were defined, how indicators were constructed, why particular specifications were chosen, and how limitations were handled.

The Data Dictionary documents the sources, variables, definitions, and coverage. The Technical Analysis presents the results. This page explains the decisions and procedures behind them.

Research Design

Research questions

The analysis addresses two related questions:

  1. Was greater pre-pandemic exposure to international visitor spending associated with more severe economic contractions in 2020?
  2. Was pre-pandemic tourism exposure associated with the extent of economic recovery by 2024?

The unit of analysis is the economy. The study covers candidate small island developing states (SIDS) across the Caribbean, Pacific, and Atlantic, Indian Ocean, and South China Sea (AIMS) regions.

Analytical approach

This is an exploratory, cross-sectional analysis. The shock analysis uses bivariate ordinary least squares (OLS) regression to examine the relationship between a pre-pandemic tourism indicator and annual real GDP growth in 2020. Pearson and Spearman correlations provide complementary measures of linear association and rank correspondence.

The recovery analysis uses indexed real GDP and real GDP per capita to compare each economy’s output with its 2019 level.

The models are deliberately bivariate. They do not control for fiscal capacity, economic structure, pre-existing economic conditions, pandemic restrictions, policy responses, or other factors that may have influenced the economic shock and recovery.

The purpose is to describe the cross-country patterns, not to isolate the independent effect of tourism. The analysis identifies associations; it does not establish causation.

Candidate Universe and Classification

Building the universe

The candidate universe was constructed from the UN Office of the High Representative for the Least Developed Countries, Landlocked Developing Countries and Small Island Developing States (UN-OHRLLS) list of SIDS, retrieved November 2026.

It contains 58 economies:

Classification Economies
OHRLLS-listed SIDS 39
Associate Member SIDS 18
Other island economy 1
Total 58

The classification preserves several distinctions:

  • OHRLLS-listed SIDS: Includes the Cook Islands and Niue, which appear on the SIDS list despite not being UN General Assembly members.
  • Associate Member SIDS: Includes associate members of UN Regional Commissions, most of which are non-sovereign territories.
  • Other island economy: Bonaire, Sint Eustatius, and Saba (BES) are included for regional completeness, but are not classified as a SIDS group member.

Bahrain was excluded because it does not appear on the current OHRLLS list.

From candidates to observations

The candidate universe is not the same as the analytical panel. Economies enter the panel when at least one source provides observations. The resulting panel contains 57 economies; BES has no rows in the retrieved sources.

The shock-analysis samples are then determined by tourism-indicator availability and 2020 GDP growth. These are data-driven inclusion rules, not judgments about which economies are substantively representative.

Sample Construction

Fixed membership

Sample membership is defined using the availability of the relevant tourism indicator during 2017–2019 and 2020 GDP growth.

The 2017–2019 period is used to establish membership because it is the most recent three-year pre-pandemic window. Once defined, membership remains fixed when the alternative 2015–2019 baseline is calculated. This prevents changes in sample composition from being mistaken for differences between baseline specifications.

Sample hierarchy

Sample Inclusion rule N
Candidate universe All candidate economies 58
Analytical panel At least one observation in any source 57
Tourism-data sample At least one tourism indicator available in 2017–2019 48
OWID shock sample OWID visitor spending and 2020 GDP growth available 29
WDI shock sample WDI tourism receipts and 2020 GDP growth available 35
Common shock sample Both tourism indicators and 2020 GDP growth available 24
Recovery sample OWID shock sample with 2019 and subsequent real GDP data 29

The two indicator-specific shock samples overlap but are not identical. The common sample provides a way to compare the indicators while holding the economies constant.

Economies outside the shock samples are excluded because of missing tourism baselines, missing 2020 GDP growth, or both. The exclusions are documented in data/processed/country_coverage.csv.

The recovery sample follows the primary OWID shock sample. All 29 economies have a 2019 real GDP observation, although coverage declines in later years.

Constructing Tourism Baselines

Indicators

Two indicators are used to represent different dimensions of international tourism exposure:

  • International visitor spending as a percentage of GDP, from Our World in Data (OWID).
  • International tourism receipts as a percentage of total exports, from the World Bank’s World Development Indicators (WDI).

These measures have different denominators and are not interchangeable. Neither is a direct measure of tourism’s contribution to domestic value added. They are treated as exposure proxies, not as estimates of tourism’s share of GDP.

Baseline windows

Two pre-pandemic windows are calculated:

Specification Years Purpose
Primary 2015–2019 Broader measure of pre-pandemic exposure
Sensitivity 2017–2019 More recent pre-pandemic conditions

The longer window provides a broader average that is less dependent on any single year. The shorter window focuses on conditions immediately preceding the pandemic.

An important qualification: The 2015–2019 primary window was evaluated after the 2017–2019 results had been examined. The primary specification was not preregistered or selected before inspecting the data. Both windows are reported in full rather than presenting only the more favorable result.

Calculation

For each economy and indicator, the baseline is the arithmetic mean of the available annual observations within the selected window:

\[ \bar{x}_{i} = \frac{1}{n_i}\sum_{t \in T_i} x_{it} \]

where \(x_{it}\) is the annual indicator value for economy \(i\), and \(T_i\) is the set of available years within the baseline window.

No missing annual values are filled. An economy with four available years in a five-year window receives a four-year mean, not an imputed five-year average. The number of observations contributing to each baseline is retained for coverage assessment.

Thin baselines

A baseline based on only one annual observation is flagged as thin. Kiribati is the only economy with a one-observation baseline in the primary OWID shock sample, and it is also thin in the shorter OWID window.

Thin baselines are retained in the principal models rather than excluded by default. Their influence is examined separately through a coverage-restricted sensitivity analysis.

Modeling the 2020 Shock

Outcome and explanatory variables

The outcome is annual real GDP growth in 2020, measured using WDI indicator NY.GDP.MKTP.KD.ZG.

The explanatory variable is the pre-pandemic tourism baseline, measured separately using each of the two indicators.

The estimated model is:

\[ GDPGrowth_{i,2020} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 TourismExposure_i + \epsilon_i \]

The slope \(\beta_1\) describes the average change in 2020 GDP growth associated with a one-percentage-point increase in the tourism exposure indicator.

For the OWID measure, this means a one-percentage-point increase in visitor spending relative to GDP. For the WDI measure, it means a one-percentage-point increase in tourism receipts relative to total exports.

The slope is an association across economies, not a causal effect.

Eight principal specifications

The models vary along three dimensions:

Dimension Specifications
Tourism indicator OWID visitor spending; WDI tourism receipts
Sample Indicator-specific sample; common sample
Baseline window 2015–2019; 2017–2019

This produces eight principal models: four for each indicator, using its own sample and the common 24-economy sample across both baseline windows.

The common-sample specifications are particularly useful because they hold country membership constant when comparing the two indicators. Differences in results are therefore less likely to be explained simply by different sample composition.

Statistical reporting

Each model reports:

  • Sample size
  • Estimated slope
  • 95% confidence interval
  • R-squared
  • Pearson correlation
  • Spearman rank correlation
  • Raw p-value
  • Holm-adjusted p-value

Holm’s step-down procedure is applied across the family of eight principal model p-values. This provides a multiple-testing adjustment while retaining the complete set of specifications. Because the models share economies and related explanatory variables, their tests are not independent; the adjustment should be interpreted in that context.

Sensitivity and Influence Diagnostics

Small cross-sectional samples can be sensitive to individual observations. Two checks are used to examine that risk.

Influential observations

Cook’s distance is calculated for each principal regression. The conventional reference threshold of \(4/N\) is used to flag potentially influential observations.

The most influential economy is then excluded in a diagnostic rerun of the relevant model. These exclusions are reported separately and do not replace the principal estimates.

This distinction matters. An influential observation is not necessarily erroneous. The Maldives, for example, represents a genuine case of exceptionally high tourism exposure. Removing it from the primary analysis would remove an economically meaningful part of the phenomenon being studied.

The exclusion checks therefore ask whether the estimated relationship depends disproportionately on a particular economy, not whether that economy should be removed from the study.

Coverage-restricted models

A second sensitivity analysis restricts each model to economies with at least three annual observations in the relevant baseline window.

This tests whether thin baselines materially affect the results. It also reveals the cost of uneven source coverage.

The most consequential case is the WDI 2017–2019 full-sample specification. Requiring three annual observations reduces the sample from 35 to 21 economies. Fourteen economies are excluded because their available WDI receipts observations are insufficient for the restriction.

The restricted model remains negative, but its statistical evidence is weaker. This result must be interpreted alongside the substantial reduction in sample size and the uneven availability of the underlying indicator.

The coverage restriction is a diagnostic choice, not a claim that three observations guarantee a representative baseline.

Constructing the Recovery Index

Index calculation

Recovery is measured using two WDI series:

  • Aggregate real GDP: NY.GDP.MKTP.KD
  • Real GDP per capita: NY.GDP.PCAP.KD

Each series is indexed independently to its 2019 value:

\[ Index_{i,t} = \left(\frac{Value_{i,t}}{Value_{i,2019}}\right)\times 100 \]

An index of 100 indicates a return to the 2019 level. Values above 100 indicate output greater than the 2019 level; values below 100 indicate output that remains lower.

Because the index is calculated relative to each economy’s own 2019 value, the comparison concerns recovery to a pre-pandemic level, not the absolute size of economies relative to one another.

Why 2019?

2019 is used as the reference year because it is the last full calendar year before the pandemic disrupted international travel. Earlier observations from 2015–2018 are retained to show the pre-pandemic trajectory.

Why 2024?

The primary recovery comparison uses 2024, the most recent year with near-complete coverage across the recovery sample. Twenty-eight of the 29 economies have an available observation; the U.S. Virgin Islands does not.

Data for 2025 are available for 22 economies, but those observations are preliminary and cover a smaller subset. They are retained where available and flagged as preliminary, but are not used for the principal cross-economy recovery comparison.

Aggregate and per-capita measures

Aggregate GDP and GDP per capita are calculated separately because they answer different questions.

Aggregate GDP measures the total output of an economy. GDP per capita adjusts for population and provides a measure of output relative to the number of residents.

The two can move in different directions. An economy can regain its previous total output while remaining below its previous output per person, or recover output per person while its total economy remains smaller.

Population growth, decline, and migration may help explain these differences, but the index alone cannot identify the mechanisms. The analysis therefore reports both measures without attributing their divergence to a specific cause.

Missing Data and Assumptions

The analysis uses available observations without filling gaps through statistical imputation.

Situation Treatment
Missing annual tourism observations Baseline calculated from available years
No tourism observations in a baseline window No baseline; excluded from that indicator’s shock sample
Missing 2020 GDP growth Excluded from shock models
Missing 2019 real GDP No recovery index can be calculated
Missing later-year GDP Economy omitted from that year’s recovery comparison
Source-published preliminary estimates Retained as supplied and flagged where applicable

No missing observations are imputed by this analysis. Source-published estimates are retained as supplied; their preliminary status is made explicit where relevant.

Several assumptions and limitations follow from these choices:

  • The two tourism indicators are treated as proxies for economic exposure, not direct measures of tourism value added.
  • Annual GDP growth captures the calendar-year contraction, not the timing or duration of restrictions within each economy.
  • The cross-sectional models do not account for differences in economic structure, policy response, fiscal capacity, or pre-existing economic conditions.
  • The baseline averages vary in the number of annual observations contributing to them.
  • The recovery index measures national output relative to 2019, not household welfare, employment quality, inequality, or the distribution of recovery gains.

These constraints define what the analysis can reasonably claim. They are not problems that can be resolved simply by adding more decimal places to the estimates.

Reproducibility

Software and packages

The analysis was conducted in R 4.4.1 and rendered using Quarto.

Principal packages include:

tidyr, dplyr, wbstats, scales, here, knitr, and ggplot2.

Script execution

The project separates acquisition, quality assessment, exploratory analysis, shock modeling, and recovery analysis into sequential scripts:

source("R/01_data_acquisition.R")
source("R/02_data_quality.R")
source("R/03_preliminary_eda.R")
source("R/04_shock_models.R")
source("R/05_recovery_analysis.R")

The acquisition script retrieves WDI observations through wbstats::wb_data(). Subsequent scripts construct the analytical panel, evaluate coverage, calculate baselines, estimate the models, run sensitivity checks, and construct recovery indices.

Rendering

The Quarto project is rendered with:

quarto render

The resulting site is published from the docs/ directory through GitHub Pages.

Data retrieval and updates

The WDI retrieval date and returned indicator codes are recorded in:

data/raw/worldbank/wdi_retrieval_metadata.txt

OWID CSV files in data/raw/ are fixed downloads from 2026 and do not update automatically. Updated OWID data require a fresh download and replacement of the local files.

WDI data are retrieved through the World Bank API and may change between runs as source data are revised.

Processed datasets, source metadata, and the project scripts are retained in the repository to support inspection and reproduction of the analysis.
